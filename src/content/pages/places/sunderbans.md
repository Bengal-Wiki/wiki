The **Sundarbans** is a cluster of low-lying islands in the Bay of Bengal, spread across India and Bangladesh, famous for its unique mangrove forests. It is the largest contiguous halophytic mangrove forest in the world, covering an area of approximately 10,000 square kilometers (3,900 sq mi). 

The name *Sundarban* literally translates to "beautiful forest" in the Bengali language, derived from the **Sundari tree** (*Heritiera fomes*), which grows in large numbers in these saline mangrove environments. The region is globally famous as the sole mangrove habitat of the **Royal Bengal Tiger** (*Panthera tigris tigris*), as well as a haven for diverse wildlife, including saltwater crocodiles, river dolphins, and hundreds of bird species.

## Geography and Ecology

The Sundarbans is formed by the confluence of three major rivers: the **Ganges**, the **Brahmaputra**, and the **Meghna**, which deposit enormous amounts of silt into the Bay of Bengal, creating a massive delta. 

The forest consists of a complex network of tidal waterways, mudflats, and small islands. Twice a day, the tides submerge large portions of the forest, creating a highly dynamic environment. 

### Unique Adaptations

The plant species in the Sundarbans have evolved fascinating survival mechanisms to cope with saline water and oxygen-deficient mud:
* **Pneumatophores (Breathing Roots)**: Since the muddy soil lacks oxygen, trees like the Sundari grow vertical roots that rise up out of the mud and water to breathe oxygen directly from the air during low tides.
* **Vivipary**: To prevent seeds from being washed away by the heavy tides, many mangrove species exhibit vivipary, where seeds germinate and develop into seedlings while still attached to the parent tree before falling.

## Flora and Fauna

The Sundarbans is a biodiversity hotspot of global significance.

### The Royal Bengal Tiger

Unlike tigers in other parts of the world, the Sundarbans tigers have adapted to life in a saline, aquatic environment. They are excellent swimmers, regularly crossing channels up to a mile wide. Due to the muddy terrain and lack of easily stalkable prey, they are known to be highly active, territorial, and historically feared as man-eaters, a behavior attributed to their harsh saline habitat.

### Other Wildlife

The ecosystem supports:
* **Reptiles**: The massive Saltwater Crocodile (*Crocodylus porosus*), King Cobras, and Rock Pythons.
* **Mammals**: Spotted Deer (*Chital*), wild boars, rhesus macaques, and the endangered Ganges River Dolphin.
* **Avifauna**: Over 250 species of birds, including the masking finfoot, Brahminy kites, and numerous kingfishers.

## Environmental Threats and Conservation

The Sundarbans acts as a vital natural barrier, absorbing the impact of severe tropical cyclones arising from the Bay of Bengal and protecting Kolkata and coastal villages from devastating tidal waves. 

However, the ecoregion faces severe threats:
1. **Climate Change & Sea Level Rise**: Several low-lying islands are already submerging under water, displacing thousands of local inhabitants (often referred to as "climate refugees").
2. **Salinity Increase**: Upstream dams (such as the Farakka Barrage in India) have reduced the flow of fresh water, making the channels increasingly saline, which causes a "top-dying" disease in Sundari trees.
3. **Pollution**: Oil spills from shipping channels and chemical runoff from industrial hubs threaten marine life and mangrove health.
