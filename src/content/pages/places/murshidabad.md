**Murshidabad** is a historic city and municipality on the eastern bank of the Bhagirathi River (a distributary of the Ganges) in the Indian state of West Bengal. 

During the 18th century, Murshidabad served as the **capital of the independent Nawabs of Bengal**, controlling a vast, wealthy territory that included modern West Bengal, Bangladesh, Bihar, and Orissa. At its peak, the city was a global center of trade and wealth, compared by British administrator Robert Clive to London for its sheer size and riches. 

Today, it is a quiet historical town, dotted with grand palaces, historic mosques, and ancient ruins that bear witness to the rise and fall of dynasties.

## History of the Capital

The city was originally a small trading post known as Makhsudabad. 

In 1704, **Nawab Murshid Quli Khan**, the Diwan (treasurer) of Bengal appointed by Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb, shifted the administrative capital of the province from Dhaka to Makhsudabad to secure it against naval attacks. He renamed the city **Murshidabad** after himself. 

Under Murshid Quli Khan and his successors (Alivardi Khan and Siraj-ud-Daulah), Murshidabad became the financial heart of the Mughal Empire, exporting fine silks, muslin, and spices globally. 

The city's golden era came to a tragic end in 1757 following Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah's defeat at the **Battle of Plassey**. The British East India Company installed a puppet Nawab, Mir Jafar, and slowly stripped the city of its wealth and administrative status, eventually shifting the capital to Calcutta in 1772.

## Key Historic Monuments

Murshidabad hosts several grand architectural structures that showcase European-Nawabi fusion styles.

### 1. Hazarduari Palace (Palace of a Thousand Doors)

Built between 1829 and 1837 by architect Duncan McLeod for Nawab Nazim Humayun Jah, this massive Greek-Doric style palace stands on the banks of the Bhagirathi River. 
* It is named **Hazarduari** because it has **one thousand doors**, of which 900 are false doors built to confuse intruders and security threats.
* The palace is now a world-famous museum, displaying a vast collection of royal weapons, oil paintings, crystal chandeliers, and rare manuscripts (including an imperial Quran written by Harun al-Rashid).

### 2. Katra Mosque

Built by Nawab Murshid Quli Khan between 1723 and 1724, this imposing brick mosque was designed as a major center of Islamic learning (Madrasa). It features two massive 70-foot octagonal corner minarets. Underneath the entrance stairs of the mosque lies the **tomb of Murshid Quli Khan**, placed there under his own wishes so that he could receive the dust of the feet of all devotees who walked into the mosque.

### 3. Nizamat Imambara

Located opposite the Hazarduari Palace, this is the largest **Imambara** (congregation hall for Shia rituals) in India, measuring over 680 feet in length. It was built in 1847 to replace an older wooden Imambara built by Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah which was destroyed in a fire.

## Murshidabad Silk and Crafts

The town is still famous for **Murshidabad Silk Sarees**, woven by local handloom weavers using high-quality mulberry silk. The silk is renowned for its lightweight, soft texture, and detailed block-printed or hand-painted borders showing traditional motifs.
