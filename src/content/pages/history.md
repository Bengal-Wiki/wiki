The **History of Bengal** spans over four millennia, characterized by powerful dynasties, rich trade connections, intellectual awakenings, and profound political transformations. Located at the apex of the Bay of Bengal, the region comprises West Bengal (an Indian state) and Bangladesh (a sovereign nation). Its history is a tale of cultural synthesis, maritime trade, artistic excellence, and pioneering struggles for freedom.

## Ancient Era

The earliest human settlements in Bengal date back to the Stone Age. Archaeological excavations at Chandraketugarh and Pandu Rajar Dhibi have uncovered evidence of an advanced chalcolithic urban culture trading with Rome and Southeast Asia.

### The Gangaridai Kingdom

According to ancient Greek, Roman, and Sanskrit records, a powerful empire known as **Gangaridai** dominated the delta region of Bengal in the 4th century BCE. The military strength of Gangaridai, particularly their fearsome elephant cavalry, is widely reported by historians like Diodorus Siculus and Plutarch as the primary reason why **Alexander the Great's** battle-weary troops refused to march further east into India, leading to his retreat from the Indus River.

### The Pala Empire (8th–12th Century)

Following centuries of fragmentation, the **Pala Dynasty** arose in the mid-8th century, founded by **Gopala**, who was democratically elected by regional chieftains to end a period of civil war (*Matsyanyaya*). 

The Palas were devout Buddhists and built massive monastic universities (Mahaviharas), including **Somapura Mahavihara** (in modern Bangladesh) and Vikramashila (in Bihar). They expanded their empire across northern India and established close cultural, trade, and religious ties with Tibet, Sumatra, and Southeast Asia. The Pala period represents a golden era of Bengali art, sculpture, and early literary development.

## Medieval Era and the Sultanate

In 1204 CE, Bakhtiyar Khalji, a general of the Delhi Sultanate, invaded Bengal, initiating centuries of Islamic rule.

### The Independent Bengal Sultanate (1352–1576)

In 1352, **Iliyas Shah** established the **Bengal Sultanate**, uniting the three major regional principalities of Sonargaon, Lakhnauti, and Satgaon under a single independent crown. 

The Sultanate was a cosmopolitan maritime empire. Rulers like Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah established direct diplomatic relations with the Ming Dynasty of China and hosted Chinese explorer **Zheng He**. The Sultanate was a period of intense architectural development, introducing the distinct curved roofs and terracotta panel designs to brick mosques, such as the massive Adina Mosque in Pandua.

### The Mughal Era (1576–1757)

Following the Battle of Rajmahal in 1576, the Mughal Empire under Akbar annexed Bengal, converting it into the **Bengal Subah** (province). 

Referred to as the *"Paradise of Nations"* due to its immense wealth and agricultural abundance, Mughal Bengal accounted for nearly 12% of the world's GDP and was the global center of textiles, producing the world's finest hand-woven **Muslin** fabric. **Dhaka** was made the capital of the province in 1608, growing into a bustling megacity of trade, filled with grand Mughal gardens, caravanserais, and mosques.

## The Colonial Era

By the early 18th century, as the central Mughal authority decayed, the Nawabs of Bengal ruled independently from Murshidabad.

### The Battle of Plassey (1757)

On 23 June 1757, the British East India Company, led by **Robert Clive**, fought the young Nawab **Siraj-ud-Daulah** at the **Battle of Plassey**. Due to the treason of the Nawab's commander, Mir Jafar, the British won a decisive victory. 

This battle was a monumental turning point in world history, establishing the British East India Company as a sovereign ruler and marking the beginning of nearly two centuries of British colonial domination over India. The wealth plundered from Bengal served as a primary source of capital that funded the **Industrial Revolution** in Britain.

### The Bengal Renaissance

In the 19th century, Calcutta, as the capital of British India, became the center of a profound cultural, social, and intellectual movement known as the **Bengal Renaissance**. 

Led by reformers like **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** (who fought to abolish Sati), **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar**, and polymaths like **Rabindranath Tagore** and **Swami Vivekananda**, the movement modernized Bengali society, promoted women's education, challenged orthodox religious dogmas, and laid the foundations for modern Indian nationalism.

## Modern Era and Partitions

Bengal's modern history is marked by painful divisions.

1. **The Partition of Bengal (1905)**: Viceroy Lord Curzon partitioned Bengal along religious lines to weaken the nationalist movement. Due to intense, unified protests by Bengalis (the *Bangabhanga* movement), the partition was annulled in 1911, but the capital of British India was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.
2. **The Partition of India (1947)**: At independence, Bengal was permanently divided. The Hindu-majority western part became **West Bengal** (a state in India), and the Muslim-majority eastern part became East Bengal (later East Pakistan, a province of Pakistan).
3. **The Bangladesh Liberation War (1971)**: Following decades of cultural and economic exploitation by West Pakistan, the people of East Pakistan launched a war of independence, led by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman. Supported by India, they defeated the Pakistani army, leading to the birth of the sovereign nation of **Bangladesh** on 16 December 1971.
